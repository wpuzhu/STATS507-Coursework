\begin{abstract}
Unsupervised analysis of multicenter radiomic data can reveal tumor phenotypes, but it can equally recover technical differences between imaging centers. We analyze T1-, T2- and ADC-derived radiomic features of 403 endometrial-cancer patients from two centers with a pipeline that combines center harmonization, a multi-view latent representation and stability-based clustering, followed by post-hoc characterization with clinical, imaging-report and pathology variables. We find that center~2 consists of two technically distinct acquisition batches, one of which contains no patient with lymphovascular invasion (LVI). ComBat harmonization by hospital center leaves most of the batch structure in place, whereas ComBat by acquisition batch removes the location and scale batch effects (batch PERMANOVA $R^2$ from 0.35 to below 0.001), although differences in the covariance structure remain. Multi-Omics Factor Analysis (MOFA+) of the harmonized data yields eight factors that explain 45--48\% of the variance of each sequence. $k$-means clustering of the factor scores, with $k$ selected from the silhouette width, subsampling stability (adjusted Rand index) and consensus clustering (proportion of ambiguous clustering), gives a weak and only moderately stable three-cluster partition (silhouette 0.23, ARI 0.74, PAC 0.50) that still partly reflects acquisition batch. No clinical or pathological variable robustly explains the clusters; the only consistent association is a lower radiologist-measured ADC value in one cluster. Cross-file consistency checks further suggest that patient linkage should be verified. These results show how batch structure and its confounding with the outcome can dominate unsupervised radiomic analyses, and they motivate harmonization on recorded acquisition metadata and external validation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Lymphovascular invasion (LVI) is a major prognostic factor in endometrial cancer and enters the risk groups that determine the extent of lymph-node staging and the indication for adjuvant therapy \cite{concin2021esgo}. Because LVI is a microscopic feature that can only be confirmed on the surgical specimen, there is interest in whether quantitative features extracted from preoperative MRI carry indirect information about it \cite{luo2020multiparametric,long2021mri,liu2024multimodal,ma2025predictive}. Our companion Supervised Report studies LVI prediction directly. Here we take an unsupervised view and ask whether the multi-sequence radiomic data contain stable patient subgroups, and whether such subgroups correspond to clinical or pathological characteristics.

Two obstacles make this question difficult. First, the data come from two centers, and radiomic features are sensitive to scanner, protocol and extraction settings, so an unsupervised method may recover imaging centers rather than tumor phenotypes \cite{mali2021radiomics,fortin2018cortical}. Second, the T1, T2 and ADC sequences provide related but nonredundant high-dimensional descriptions of the same tumor, which calls for a representation that distinguishes variation shared across sequences from variation specific to one of them.

We address these obstacles with a sequential pipeline: center harmonization with ComBat and its extensions, evaluated with explicit tests; a multi-view latent representation with MOFA+; clustering of the latent factor scores with the number of clusters chosen by internal validation and resampling stability; and post-hoc characterization of the clusters. Our main contributions are (i) the detection of two hidden acquisition batches within one center, one of which is confounded with the outcome; (ii) a systematic comparison of harmonization strategies at the feature, latent-factor and cluster level; and (iii) a stability-based clustering and post-hoc analysis that separates technical from clinically interpretable structure.

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Radiomics and Center Harmonization}

Radiomics provides a quantitative characterization of tumor phenotype through high-dimensional features describing intensity, shape, texture, and spatial heterogeneity \cite{gillies2016radiomics}. In multi-sequence MRI, the imaging sequences offer related but nonredundant descriptions of the same tumor. T1- and T2-weighted images characterize different aspects of anatomy and tissue composition, whereas apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) images quantify water diffusion and may provide information related to tumor cellularity. Joint analysis of these complementary measurements can therefore provide a more complete description of tumor heterogeneity than separate analyses of the individual sequences \cite{subramanian2020multiomics,vahabi2022unsupervised}.

A central challenge in multicenter radiomic analysis is that extracted features reflect technical as well as biological variation. Scanner manufacturer, magnetic-field strength, acquisition and reconstruction parameters, voxel size, segmentation procedures, and intensity preprocessing can affect feature distributions and reproducibility \cite{mali2021radiomics,zwanenburg2020ibsi}. Consequently, directly pooling radiomic features across centers can introduce systematic location and scale differences. When these technical effects are large, unsupervised dimension reduction and clustering may primarily distinguish imaging centers rather than clinically meaningful tumor phenotypes; multisite MRI studies have similarly shown that scanner effects can dominate the major directions of variation \cite{fortin2018cortical,leek2010tackling}.

ComBat was originally introduced as an empirical Bayes method for correcting batch effects in high-dimensional genomic data \cite{johnson2007combat}. It models each feature as
\begin{equation}
    x_{ij} = \alpha_j + \mathbf{c}_i^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta_j + \gamma_{gj} + \delta_{gj}\varepsilon_{ij},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_j$ is the overall mean of feature $j$, $\mathbf{c}_i$ are optional covariates to be protected, and $\gamma_{gj}$ and $\delta_{gj}$ are the additive and multiplicative effects of batch $g$, estimated by empirical Bayes while borrowing strength across features. ComBat was subsequently adapted to diffusion and structural MRI \cite{fortin2017dti,fortin2018cortical} and to PET and CT radiomics, where it reduced center-associated differences while retaining patient-specific variation and supported more reproducible multicenter models \cite{orlhac2018postreconstruction,daano2020combat,masson2021harmonization}. Practical guidance stresses that the batch must be the acquisition setting that actually generates the technical shift, not merely the hospital, and that harmonization should be checked feature by feature and on the joint distribution \cite{orlhac2022guide}.

Two limitations of ComBat are central to this work. First, it aligns only the first two moments of each feature. CovBat additionally harmonizes the between-feature covariance \cite{chen2022covbat}, and generalized or nested ComBat handles multimodal feature distributions and several overlapping batch effects, including batches that are not known in advance \cite{horng2022generalized}. Second, when batch is confounded with the biological variable of interest, protecting that variable in the ComBat model re-injects group differences and inflates downstream significance \cite{nygaard2016methods}. The single-cell literature makes the same point for normalization: technical scale factors are not identifiable from biology without design assumptions, and normalization choices can create or erase apparent structure \cite{vallejos2017normalizing}.

\subsection{Latent Representation Learning}

Harmonization alone does not integrate information across MRI sequences. After center correction, the T1, T2, and ADC measurements remain separate high-dimensional feature matrices. Direct feature concatenation is a simple integration strategy, but it can allow a modality with more features or greater residual variance to dominate the analysis, and it does not distinguish variation shared across modalities from variation specific to one modality. These limitations motivate structured latent-variable methods for multimodal integration \cite{li2026integrative}.

The iCluster framework introduced a joint latent-variable model linking multiple genomic data types through common sample-level factors \cite{shen2009icluster}. Extensions incorporated heterogeneous outcome distributions and sparse feature selection \cite{mo2013pattern}, as well as fully Bayesian inference \cite{mo2018bayesian}. Similarly, Joint and Individual Variation Explained (JIVE) decomposes linked datasets into joint structure, modality-specific structure, and residual noise \cite{lock2013jive}; clustering the joint and individual components can reveal shared and modality-specific subgroups, respectively \cite{hellton2016integrative}.

Multi-Omics Factor Analysis (MOFA) provides a general probabilistic multi-view factor model. Although it was originally developed for multi-omics data, its formulation is applicable to other aligned, high-dimensional modalities, including radiomic feature sets. For modality $m$, the model can be summarized as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X}^{(m)} = \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{W}^{(m)\mathsf{T}} + \mathbf{E}^{(m)},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{Z}$ contains patient-level factor scores, $\mathbf{W}^{(m)}$ contains modality-specific feature weights, and $\mathbf{E}^{(m)}$ contains residual variation. Sparsity-inducing and automatic relevance determination priors allow a factor to explain variation shared by several modalities or variation concentrated in a single modality \cite{argelaguet2018mofa}. MOFA+ extends this framework with more scalable variational inference and group-wise regularization \cite{argelaguet2020mofaplus}. It can also accommodate partially overlapping sample sets and missing observations; nevertheless, reliable cross-modal integration requires aligned sample identities.

Reviews of high-dimensional single-cell data summarize the broader toolbox: linear projections such as PCA and factor analysis for representation, and neighbor embeddings such as t-SNE and UMAP for visualization only, because their distances are not globally meaningful \cite{wu2020tools,zogopoulos2025scrnaseq,vandermaaten2008tsne}. Deep generative models such as the variational autoencoder learn nonlinear latent spaces \cite{kingma2014vae}; the deep count autoencoder replaces the Gaussian decoder with a likelihood matched to the data \cite{eraslan2019dca}, and likelihood-free VAEs use a proper scoring rule, the energy score, to avoid decoder misspecification \cite{xu2025envae}. Deep clustering methods such as ClusterGAN and Variational Deep Embedding \cite{mukherjee2019clustergan,jiang2016variational} and multi-view clustering in latent embedding spaces \cite{chen2020multi} follow the same idea. With about 400 patients and 1{,}100 correlated features, we preferred the linear, interpretable MOFA model, whose factors can be traced back to radiomic features.

\subsection{Integrative Clustering and Cluster Validation}

Integrative clustering methods identify sample subgroups by jointly analyzing multiple high-dimensional modalities \cite{li2026integrative}. One widely used strategy, adopted here, is to estimate a low-dimensional representation shared across modalities and then cluster the estimated scores \cite{shen2009icluster,hellton2016integrative}. A second family estimates related but potentially distinct clusterings for the individual modalities: Bayesian correlated clustering models dependence between modality-specific cluster memberships \cite{kirk2012bayesian}, Bayesian consensus clustering shrinks the modality-specific partitions toward an overall partition \cite{lock2013consensus}, and Clusternomics models both local and global clusters with a hierarchical Dirichlet mixture \cite{gabasova2017clusternomics}. A third family integrates sample similarities rather than features, as in Similarity Network Fusion \cite{wang2014snf}. Toolkits such as MOVICS run several integrative algorithms and combine them by consensus \cite{lu2020movics}; applied to multiparametric breast MRI radiomics, this approach defined subgroups that differed in receptor status, tumor size and nodal metastasis \cite{he2024integrative}, which is the closest analogue to this work.

Because every clustering algorithm returns a partition whether or not structure exists, the number of clusters must be justified by internal validation. The silhouette width measures separation relative to cohesion \cite{rousseeuw1987silhouettes}; resampling stability compares partitions of perturbed data with the adjusted Rand index (ARI) \cite{hubert1985comparing,lange2004stability}; and consensus clustering summarizes how often pairs of samples co-cluster across subsamples \cite{monti2003consensus}. The proportion of ambiguous clustering (PAC) of the consensus matrix is a more reliable criterion than the consensus-CDF area, which tends to favor large $k$ \cite{senbabaoglu2014critical}.

\subsection{Post-hoc Characterization}

Clusters are interpreted by comparing external variables across them. Benchmarks of marker-gene selection in single-cell data show that simple procedures (Wilcoxon-type rank tests, $t$-tests, logistic regression) are competitive with complex methods, but that testing variables against clusters estimated from the same data inflates significance, so effect sizes and replication matter more than $p$-values alone \cite{pullin2024comparison}. In the radiomics setting the external variables are not used to build the clusters, which removes that double-dipping problem. They can, however, be confounded with center, which motivates batch-adjusted and within-center analyses. Throughout, false-discovery-rate control is applied within each family of tests \cite{benjamini1995fdr}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Data and Preprocessing}

The dataset used in this study was collected by the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine from two clinical centers over a specified period, with appropriate patient consent and authorization for public use. The original MRI signals were preprocessed following a multiparametric MRI-based radiomics pipeline \cite{cao2024multiparameter}, implemented by one of the original contributors, Jingya Chen, who is in the Department of Radiology at the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine.

T1, T2 and ADC radiomic feature tables (396 features each; $n=403$ patients, 222 from center~1 and 181 from center~2) were aligned by case identifier. For each sequence separately, we removed features with more than 10\% missing values and degenerate features (a single value shared by more than 20\% of an acquisition batch, or zero within-batch interquartile range). Remaining missing values were imputed with the within-batch median. Strictly positive features were log-transformed, which turns multiplicative scale differences into additive shifts; other features were Yeo--Johnson transformed. All features were then standardized and winsorized at $\pm5$ SD. After this step 375, 376 and 344 features remained for T1, T2 and ADC.

\subsection{Acquisition Batches}

Exploratory analysis showed that center~2 is not homogeneous. For about 100 run-length and low-grey-level texture features, 99 of the 181 center-2 patients have values several orders of magnitude larger than all other patients, and the same 99 patients are flagged in T1, T2 and ADC. We scored each patient by the fraction of these features exceeding ten times the center-1 99th percentile, averaged over sequences. The score is sharply bimodal (no patient between 0.36 and 0.63), giving three acquisition batches without using the outcome: C1 ($n=222$), C2a ($n=82$) and C2b ($n=99$). All C2b patients are LVI-negative (Table~\ref{tab:ue-batch}).

\subsection{Harmonization and Its Evaluation}

We compared six strategies, each applied separately to each sequence: no correction; ComBat with hospital center as batch; ComBat with acquisition batch (primary); ComBat with acquisition batch and LVI protected; CovBat with acquisition batch \cite{chen2022covbat}; and a non-parametric within-batch rank inverse-normal transform (rank-INT). ComBat used the parametric empirical-Bayes \texttt{neuroCombat} implementation. The primary model protects no covariate, so that the outcome never enters the features \cite{nygaard2016methods}.

Harmonization was evaluated with (i) feature-wise Kruskal--Wallis (location) and Brown--Forsythe (scale) tests against batch, with BH-FDR and $\epsilon^2$ effect sizes; (ii) multivariate separability, measured by the PERMANOVA $R^2$ of batch (499 permutations), the silhouette of batch labels in the first ten principal components, and the 5-fold cross-validated AUC of a random forest predicting batch, which is sensitive to higher-order differences; and (iii) preservation of biological signal, measured by the share of LVI-associated features and the cross-validated AUC for LVI.

\subsection{Latent Representation}

Within each harmonized sequence, a greedy filter removed features with $|r|>0.9$ to an already retained feature, and the remaining features were standardized; additional view scaling was therefore disabled in MOFA. MOFA+ (\texttt{mofapy2}) was fitted with T1, T2 and ADC as three views and one group, using 10 initial factors, ARD and spike-and-slab priors on the weights, a 1\% variance-explained drop threshold, at most 1{,}000 iterations with medium convergence, and seed 507 \cite{argelaguet2020mofaplus}. Retained factors were described by their total and per-view variance explained and by their highest-weighted features.

The batch association of each factor was measured by its Kruskal--Wallis $\epsilon^2$ (location), by the ratio of its largest to smallest within-batch SD (scale), and by the random-forest AUC for batch computed from all factors. MOFA was refitted for every harmonization strategy. Two further fits served as sensitivity analyses: one excluding C2b, and one on center~1 alone, which is a single acquisition batch and is therefore free of harmonization and of the batch--outcome confounding.

\subsection{Clustering and Selection of $k$}

Standardized factor scores were clustered by $k$-means (50 starts) for $k=2,\dots,8$. For each $k$ we computed the mean silhouette and the Calinski--Harabasz and Davies--Bouldin indices. We then drew $B=200$ subsamples of 80\% of patients and re-clustered each one, recording the ARI against the full-data partition restricted to the same patients and the ARI between consecutive subsample partitions on their shared patients. We accumulated the consensus matrix $M_{ij}=\#\{\text{co-clustered}\}/\#\{\text{co-sampled}\}$ and its PAC on $(0.1,0.9)$ \cite{monti2003consensus,senbabaoglu2014critical}. Partitions whose smallest cluster held fewer than 10\% of patients were excluded; among the remaining partitions, the optimal $k$ maximized the average of the rank-normalized silhouette, mean ARI and $1-\mathrm{PAC}$.

Every candidate partition was also cross-tabulated against acquisition batch and LVI (Cram\'er's $V$). Robustness was quantified by the ARI between the primary partition and the partitions from Ward clustering, a Gaussian mixture, and every alternative harmonization (same $k$, shared patients). t-SNE embeddings of the factor scores were used for visualization only.

\subsection{Post-hoc Analysis}

Twenty-one clinical, imaging-report and pathology variables (Table~\ref{tab:ue-posthoc}) were compared across clusters. Two implausible values were set to missing: p53 $=11$ (case 314) and PR $=808\%$ (case 50). Continuous variables were compared with the Kruskal--Wallis test (effect size $\epsilon^2$), and categorical variables with the $\chi^2$ test, or a Monte-Carlo $\chi^2$ test when any expected count was below 5 (effect size Cram\'er's $V$). Each variable was also tested with a batch-adjusted likelihood-ratio test of the cluster term, in $\mathrm{rank}(y)\sim\text{cluster}+\text{batch}$ (OLS) for continuous $y$ and in logistic or multinomial regression for categorical $y$, and within center~1 alone. BH-FDR was applied across variables.

A variable was \emph{retained} if it reached $q<0.05$ both unadjusted and batch-adjusted, with at least a small effect ($\epsilon^2\ge0.01$ or $V\ge0.10$), and was a \emph{candidate} if its nominal batch-adjusted $p$ was below 0.05 with at least a small effect. The explanatory value of retained (or, if none, candidate) variables was measured by the repeated 5-fold cross-validated macro-AUC of a multinomial logistic model for cluster membership, compared with a model using all variables and one using batch alone. Clusters were also described by their mean factor scores and by the highest-weighted features of the most discriminating factors.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Data Integrity}

Table~\ref{tab:ue-integrity} reports cross-file consistency checks. For the same patient, segmented tumor volume agreed between sequences in C2a (Spearman $\rho\approx0.76$) but only weakly in C1 ($\rho\approx0.25$) and not at all in C2b ($\rho\approx0$). In no batch did radiomic volume track the radiologist-measured maximum diameter, or whole-tumor mean ADC track the radiologist ADC value. Rows are also ordered by LVI within each center, and the 30 LVI-positive center-2 patients occupy a contiguous block of case identifiers (58--87). These patterns are consistent with records that are not linked to the same patient across the T1, T2, ADC and post-hoc tables, or with different regions of interest, in parts of the cohort. All results below are conditional on the linkage as provided.

Without correction, batch explained 32--37\% of the multivariate variance within each sequence (PERMANOVA $R^2$; 35\% for the concatenated features), and 91--97\% of features differed in location between batches (Table~\ref{tab:ue-harm}, Figs.~\ref{fig:ue-harm-pca} and~\ref{fig:ue-harm-mv}). ComBat by acquisition batch reduced the batch $R^2$ to below 0.001 ($p=1.0$), the batch silhouette from 0.29 to $-0.02$, and the share of location-associated features to 5--11\%.

Fewer features still differed in scale (25--32\%), and the random forest still identified batch from the full feature set (AUC $\approx1$). The rank-INT transform removed all marginal differences (0\% of features significant), yet the random forest still reached AUC 0.96--0.98. The residual batch information is therefore carried by the joint (covariance) structure rather than by individual features, and CovBat did not remove it. Protecting LVI in ComBat raised the LVI AUC from 0.70 to 0.88, the inflation predicted by Nygaard et al.~\cite{nygaard2016methods} when the protected variable is confounded with batch; this model was therefore not used as the primary.

\subsection{MOFA Representation}

On the primary harmonized data, MOFA retained eight factors that explained 46\%, 48\% and 45\% of the variance of T1, T2 and ADC (Fig.~\ref{fig:ue-mofa}). Factor~1 was shared by all three sequences, factors 2--3 were T1-specific, factors 4 and 8 mainly T2, and factors 5--7 mainly ADC, with a T2 contribution to factors 6--7. Without correction, or with ComBat by center, one factor was almost entirely a batch factor ($\epsilon^2=0.58$ and $0.61$). After ComBat by batch, the largest factor location effect was $\epsilon^2=0.07$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ue-mofa-batch}). However, C2b patients had up to 12-fold smaller spread on the shared factors than C1 patients. Excluding C2b reduced this ratio to 2.4, and the random forest still predicted batch from the factors (AUC 0.99).

The clusters were separated mainly by factor~4 ($\epsilon^2=0.38$; T2 grey-level non-uniformity), factor~5 ($\epsilon^2=0.28$; ADC high-grey-level run emphasis) and factor~6 ($\epsilon^2=0.24$; ADC low-grey-level run emphasis and low T2 intensity percentiles). \section{Discussion}

Harmonization was decisive in this cohort, but the relevant batch was not the hospital. Center~2 combines at least two extraction or acquisition runs, and ComBat by center left them intact, in line with the recommendation that the batch should be defined by the acquisition setting \cite{orlhac2022guide}. Defining batches from the data and applying ComBat to them removed the location and scale batch effects that dominate the uncorrected principal components and MOFA factors.

Three findings nevertheless limit what the unsupervised clusters can show. First, batch effects in the covariance structure remain: they are detectable by a nonlinear classifier and visible as compressed factor scores in C2b, and neither CovBat nor a rank-based transform removed them. Generalized ComBat variants designed for multimodal distributions and multiple batch effects \cite{horng2022generalized} are a natural next step. Second, batch is confounded with LVI: C2b is entirely LVI-negative, and the center-2 LVI-positive patients form their own block. No harmonization can separate batch from outcome under this design, and protecting LVI inflates its apparent signal \cite{nygaard2016methods,leek2010tackling}. Third, the cross-file consistency checks suggest that patient linkage between sequences and the clinical table may be incorrect for part of the cohort.

Within these limits, the latent space shows weak cluster structure. The silhouette is about 0.23, and subsampling gives a moderately stable three-cluster partition, driven by T2 and ADC texture factors, that depends on the harmonization choice. Unlike integrative radiomics clustering in breast MRI, where clusters differed in receptor status, tumor size and nodal involvement \cite{he2024integrative}, our clusters are not explained by the clinical, imaging-report or pathology variables. The only consistent association is a lower radiologist ADC value in cluster~1, which also holds within center~1. Lower ADC is generally associated with higher cellularity, but the effect is small. The single-center analysis, which is free of harmonization, likewise found no LVI-related phenotype. These results agree with the Supervised Report, in which the LVI signal remaining within a single acquisition batch was modest.

\section{Conclusion}

We combined center harmonization, MOFA+ latent representation, stability-based clustering and post-hoc analysis to study the structure of multi-sequence MRI radiomics in endometrial cancer. Harmonization by acquisition batch, rather than by hospital, was needed to remove the dominant technical variation, but residual covariance effects and the confounding of batch with LVI remained. The resulting clusters are weak, only moderately stable and not explained by clinical or pathological variables, so we present them as exploratory imaging phenotypes rather than clinically validated subtypes.

Future work should (i) verify the case linkage and acquisition metadata and harmonize on recorded scanner and protocol information rather than inferred batches; (ii) re-extract features with a common discretization; (iii) evaluate harmonization methods that address covariance and multimodal batch effects; and (iv) validate any phenotype in an external cohort in which LVI is not confounded with acquisition.
